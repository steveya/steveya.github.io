## Recap

In the [first post]({% post_url 2024-07-12-volatility-forecasts-1 %}) of our volatility forecasts series, we introduced the Smooth Transition Exponential Smoothing (STES) model (Taylor, 2004) as a generalization of the simpler Exponential Smoothing (ES) model. The smoothing parameter $$\alpha$$ in ES is a constant through time, but in STES $$\alpha$$ becomes $$\alpha_t$$ and is computed from a logistic regression using features $$X_t$$.

### Notation and timing

The volatility estimate is a one-step-ahead forecast

$$
\sigma^2_{t \vert t-1} = \mathbb{E}\left[r_t^2 \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]
$$

where $$\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$$ is the information available up to time $$t-1$$. The simplest ES-style volatility filter updates the forecast using the most recently observed squared return:

$$
\sigma^2_{t \vert t-1} = (1-\alpha) \sigma^2_{t-1 \vert t-2} + \alpha r_{t-1}^2,
\qquad \alpha \in (0,1)
$$

This is a convex combination of “old belief” and “new data”, and it builds in a single exponential memory scale governed by $$\alpha$$. Large $$\alpha$$ reacts quickly; small $$\alpha$$ averages more heavily.

### STES and XGBoost-STES recap

In STES, the smoothing parameter becomes time-varying:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_t &= \mathrm{sigmoid}(w^\top X_t + b), \\
\sigma^2_{t \vert t-1} &= (1-\alpha_t) \sigma^2_{t-1 \vert t-2} + \alpha_t r_{t-1}^2
\end{aligned}
$$

where $$X_t$$ is a vector of state variables observed at (or before) $$t-1$$. The intent is simple: let the filter adapt. In calm markets, $$\alpha_t$$ can be small (slow updates). In stressed regimes, $$\alpha_t$$ can jump (fast updates).

In the [second post]({% post_url 2024-07-18-volatility-forecasts-2 %}), we generalized this idea further by letting $$\alpha_t$$ be generated by a flexible non-linear model $$\mathcal{F}$$ (e.g. XGBoost):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_t &= \mathrm{sigmoid}(\mathcal{F}(X_t)), \\
\sigma^2_{t \vert t-1} &= (1-\alpha_t) \sigma^2_{t-1 \vert t-2} + \alpha_t r_{t-1}^2
\end{aligned}
$$

Both STES and XGBoost-STES are therefore examples of adaptive exponential smoothing: the recursion is the same; only the rule for $$\alpha_t$$ changes.

This naturally raises a question:

> If ES/STES are adaptive recursive filters, how do they relate to modern sequence models like RNNs and GRUs?

In this post, we show:

- ES is a special case of a constrained (linear) RNN;
- STES is a special case of a constrained GRU (specifically, its update gate);
- and the “neural” generalization suggests a clean path to modern architectures without discarding interpretability.

## From ES to Simple RNN

### Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN)

A (vanilla) RNN maintains a hidden state $$h_t$$ summarizing the past. Given an input $$x_t$$ at time $$t$$, it updates:

$$
h_t = \phi(W_x x_t + W_h h_{t-1} + b)
$$

and optionally produces an output:

$$
\widehat{y}_t = g(h_t)
$$

Here $$\phi$$ is a nonlinearity (e.g. $$\tanh$$ or sigmoid), and the same parameters are reused at every time step. This weight sharing is what makes it a sequence model.

### Exponential Smoothing as a constrained 1D linear RNN

We can map ES into the RNN framework by choosing

- input $$x_t = r_t^2$$ (the new “measurement”),
- hidden state $$h_t = \sigma^2_{t \vert t-1}$$ (the filtered variance forecast),
- and a linear activation $$\phi(u)=u$$ because ES is linear.

Now consider the 1D (scalar) RNN update:

$$
h_t = W_x x_{t-1} + W_h h_{t-1} + b
$$

If we set $$b=0$$ and enforce the constraints

$$
W_x = \alpha,\qquad W_h = 1-\alpha,\qquad \alpha \in (0,1)
$$

we obtain

$$
h_t = \alpha x_{t-1} + (1-\alpha) h_{t-1}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\sigma^2_{t \vert t-1} = \alpha r_{t-1}^2 + (1-\alpha) \sigma^2_{t-1 \vert t-2}
$$

which is exactly exponential smoothing.

So ES is a linear RNN with a 1D hidden state and a convex-combination constraint on its weights.

### What does the RNN viewpoint buy us?

This perspective clarifies what ES is doing:

- It is a particular parameterization of memory.
- The hidden state is a summary of the past.
- ES is “single-timescale memory”: the kernel is exponential with one decay rate $$\alpha$$.

It also suggests two immediate generalizations:

1) Unconstrained linear RNN:

$$
h_t = W_x x_{t-1} + W_h h_{t-1}
$$

which is still a linear filter but no longer guarantees convex combination or stability unless we constrain $$W_h$$.

2) Nonlinear RNN:

$$
h_t = \phi(W_x x_{t-1} + W_h h_{t-1} + b)
$$

which can represent richer state dependence but is harder to interpret and can be harder to train.

STES takes a different (and, for volatility, often more interpretable) route: keep the ES recursion but make the mixing weight $$\alpha_t$$ state-dependent.

## From STES to Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)

### Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)

One of the limitations of vanilla RNNs is that learning long-range dependencies can be difficult (vanishing/exploding gradients). GRUs address this by introducing gates that explicitly control how much of the old state to retain and how much new information to incorporate.

A standard GRU (one common notation) is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
z_t &= \sigma(W_z x_t + U_z h_{t-1} + b_z) \quad &&\text{(update gate)}\\
s_t &= \sigma(W_s x_t + U_s h_{t-1} + b_s) \quad &&\text{(reset gate)}\\
\tilde{h}_t &= \tanh\!\big(W_h x_t + U_h (s_t \odot h_{t-1}) + b_h\big) \quad &&\text{(candidate state)}\\
h_t &= (1 - z_t)\odot h_{t-1} + z_t \odot \tilde{h}_t \quad &&\text{(state update)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The key line is the last one: the hidden state is a convex interpolation between the previous state and a candidate update, with the interpolation weight given by the update gate $$z_t$$.

### STES as a special case of a GRU update

Recall STES:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_t &= \sigma(w^\top X_t + b), \\
\sigma^2_{t \vert t-1} &= (1-\alpha_t) \sigma^2_{t-1 \vert t-2} + \alpha_t r_{t-1}^2
\end{aligned}
$$

Compare this with the GRU update:

$$
h_t = (1-z_t)h_{t-1} + z_t \tilde{h}_t
$$

We can identify:

$$
h_t \equiv \sigma^2_{t \vert t-1},\qquad 
z_t \equiv \alpha_t,\qquad
\tilde{h}_t \equiv r_{t-1}^2
$$

So the only thing left to show is that a GRU can be constrained so that:

1) its update gate $$z_t$$ depends only on features $$X_t$$ (like STES), and  
2) its candidate $$\tilde{h}_t$$ is exactly the last squared return.

#### Step 1: make the gate match STES

If we remove dependence on $$h_{t-1}$$ by setting $$U_z = 0$$, and feed $$x_t = X_t$$, then:

$$
z_t = \sigma(W_z X_t + b_z)
$$

which matches the STES form $$\alpha_t = \sigma(w^\top X_t + b)$$ (in the 1D case we can view $$W_z$$ as a row vector).

This is an important conceptual point:

- STES is a “purely exogenous gate”: $$\alpha_t$$ depends on $$X_t$$, not on the previous filtered variance.
- A full GRU is richer: it can make the gate depend on both $$X_t$$ and $$h_{t-1}$$.

#### Step 2: force the candidate to be $$r_{t-1}^2$$

Now we want $$\tilde{h}_t = r_{t-1}^2$$. One simple way is to include $$r_{t-1}^2$$ as a component of the GRU input. Let

$$
x_t =
\begin{bmatrix}
r_{t-1}^2\\
X_t
\end{bmatrix}
$$

If we replace the candidate nonlinearity with identity (linear activation) and constrain parameters so that the candidate picks out only the first component:

$$
\tilde{h}_t = 
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0\end{bmatrix}x_t
= r_{t-1}^2
$$

then the GRU update becomes:

$$
h_t = (1-z_t)h_{t-1} + z_t r_{t-1}^2
$$

which is exactly the STES recursion with $$z_t=\alpha_t$$:

$$
\sigma^2_{t \vert t-1} = (1-\alpha_t) \sigma^2_{t-1 \vert t-2} + \alpha_t r_{t-1}^2
$$

In words: STES is a GRU where the update gate is the time-varying smoothing parameter, and the candidate state is “just use last period’s squared return”.

### Why this equivalence matters

This is more than a cute mapping. It tells us what STES is doing in neural-network language:

- STES is a one-dimensional gated state-space model.
- The logistic regression in STES is literally an update gate.
- ES/STES becomes a spectrum of increasingly expressive gating rules:

  - ES: constant gate $$\alpha$$  
  - STES: exogenous gate $$\alpha_t = \sigma(w^\top X_t + b)$$  
  - GRU: endogenous + exogenous gate $$\alpha_t = \sigma(W X_t + U h_{t-1} + b)$$

This also suggests a natural “neural STES” generalization that stays close to the original recursion:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_t &= \sigma(\mathcal{N}_\theta(X_t, h_{t-1})),\\
\sigma^2_{t \vert t-1} &= (1-\alpha_t) \sigma^2_{t-1 \vert t-2} + \alpha_t r_{t-1}^2
\end{aligned}
$$

where $$\mathcal{N}_\theta$$ can be a small MLP, an attention block, or even a full GRU gate. In this view, the key design choice is:

> Do we want the model’s memory update rate to depend on the current regime (features), on the internal state, or both?

### Positivity and parameterization

One practical advantage of ES/STES is that the recursion preserves non-negativity: if $$\sigma^2_{t-1 \vert t-2}\ge 0$$, $$r_{t-1}^2\ge 0$$, and $$\alpha_t\in(0,1)$$, then $$\sigma^2_{t \vert t-1}\ge 0$$.

Neural models can break this unless we parameterize carefully. Two common safe parameterizations are:

- model $$\log \sigma^2$$ and exponentiate at the end,
- or map outputs through $$\mathrm{softplus}(\cdot)$$.

In this series, we will focus on architectures that preserve the “convex interpolation” structure because it keeps the model stable and interpretable.

## Training Objective

To train any of the models above, we need to specify (i) a target, and (ii) a loss function.

### Targets

The most direct target is the next-period squared return:

$$
y_t = r_t^2
$$

In practice, for daily data, $$r_t^2$$ is a very noisy proxy for latent variance. If intraday data is available, we can replace $$r_t^2$$ with a realized variance proxy $$RV_t$$, but the conceptual story stays the same.

### Loss functions

A simple choice is mean squared error (MSE):

$$
\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{MSE}(\theta) = \sum_{t=t_0}^T \left(y_t - \hat{\sigma}^2_{t \vert t-1}(\theta)\right)^2
$$

However, volatility forecasting is often evaluated with losses that are more aligned with conditional variance models. One popular option is the QLIKE loss:

$$
\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{QLIKE}(\theta) = \sum_{t=t_0}^T \left(\log \hat{\sigma}^2_{t \vert t-1}(\theta) + \frac{y_t}{\hat{\sigma}^2_{t \vert t-1}(\theta)}\right)
$$

QLIKE penalizes under-forecasting variance more strongly and is well-behaved when $$\hat{\sigma}^2_{t \vert t-1}$$ is positive. This makes it a natural fit for ES/STES-style models where positivity is structurally enforced.

In the empirical section, we will report results under both MSE and QLIKE to ensure conclusions are not an artifact of a particular metric.

## Wrapping Up

Exponential smoothing is a one-dimensional recurrent model with a convex-combination update. STES is the same recurrence, but with a state-dependent gate. GRUs generalize this exact gating pattern: the hidden state is always updated by interpolating between the old state and a candidate state.

This equivalence gives us a principled ladder of model complexity:

$$
\text{ES} \subset \text{STES} \subset \text{GRU-like gated filters}.
$$

The goal of the empirical section is not to “beat ES at all costs”, but to understand when the added flexibility (time-varying gates, richer state dependence) is actually useful for volatility forecasting—and when it simply overfits noise.

In the next post, we will combine this work with our series on the signature method. Including the signature features can potentially improve the performance of the more complex models.
