1 Introduction

Part 5 left the PGARCH branch open but unresolved. The three-channel PGARCH-L decomposition improved QLIKE relative to the earlier STES family and directionally improved on GARCH(1,1), but it did not clear the series decision rule on its own. At the same time, the earlier feature-expansion work showed that simply widening the predictor set inside a single STES gate was not enough to create a useful forecasting gain.

Part 6 sits between that simpler PGARCH result and the broader Part 7 search. Its role is narrower than a final model-selection post. This is the step where the PGARCH line is expanded beyond the Part 5 formulations and asked a specific question: if the predictor set is made materially richer, does the predictive-GARCH recursion improve once those predictors are allowed to affect different structural channels rather than one common gate?

The answer in this notebook is disciplined but mixed. The all-feature linear PGARCH variants do not beat the Part 5 PGARCH-L baseline, and none of the expanded linear variants clears GARCH(1,1) under the Part 6 protocol. That negative result matters. It shows that richer information alone is not enough. At the same time, the exercise clarifies where richer predictors plausibly belong inside the recursion, exposes the limits of regularization-only allocation, and identifies the nonlinear XGBPGARCH line as the natural next search step rather than a solved endpoint.

The notebook therefore does four things in sequence. It places Part 6 inside the series, lays out the channel-based model roadmap and evaluation standard, summarizes the forecasting evidence, and then reads the diagnostic results that motivate Part 7.

2 Why Part 6 Exists

This post is the expansion step inside the PGARCH branch of the series. Part 5 worked with a small return-based feature set and asked whether the predictive-GARCH decomposition itself helped. Part 6 keeps the dataset, target, benchmark, and train/test protocol fixed, but expands the PGARCH line along a different axis: richer predictors and richer allocation across the level \mu_t, persistence \phi_t, and innovation-share g_t channels.

That placement matters for interpretation. Part 6 is not meant to settle the full expanded-feature problem. It is an intermediate result. The goal is to see whether a more structured linear PGARCH specification can turn the earlier feature-expansion idea into a benchmark-relevant forecasting gain, or whether the branch still needs a broader search over feature compression, nonlinear channel updates, or both.

The right public reading is therefore conservative. A positive finding here would mean that the expanded PGARCH line remains active under the Part 6 protocol. A negative finding would still be useful if it clarified which types of richer structure were worth carrying into Part 7.

3 Model Roadmap

3.1 The PGARCH Recursion

The PGARCH model defines the one-step-ahead variance forecast as

h_{t+1} = (1 - \phi_t)\,\mu_t + \phi_t\,q_t, \qquad q_t = g_t\,y_t + (1 - g_t)\,h_t

where y_t \ge 0 is the realized target, h_t is the current variance forecast, and the three structural parameters are interpreted as follows. The level channel \mu_t sets the anchor toward which variance mean-reverts. The persistence channel \phi_t controls how strongly the recursion leans on that anchor versus the internal update. The innovation-share channel g_t controls how aggressively new information is absorbed into the recursion.

In PGARCH-L, each channel is a linear function of predictors passed through a link that enforces admissible values:

\mu_t = \mu_{\min} + \text{softplus}(\tilde{x}_{t-1}^\top w_\mu), \qquad \phi_t = \phi_{\min} + (\phi_{\max} - \phi_{\min})\,\sigma(\tilde{x}_{t-1}^\top w_\phi), \qquad g_t = g_{\min} + (g_{\max} - g_{\min})\,\sigma(\tilde{x}_{t-1}^\top w_g)

The important modeling detail for Part 6 is that the current PGARCHLinearModel passes the same feature matrix to all three channels. Differential shrinkage is therefore a disciplined first attempt at channel allocation, not a hard architectural separation.

3.2 The Part 6 Ladder

The progression into the Part 6 models is simple and deliberate.

Step Model What changes Why it is in scope
1 Linear PGARCH-L baseline Reuse the Part 5 three-feature specification Anchor the expanded-feature experiment to the accepted family baseline
2 Expanded linear PGARCH with uniform shrinkage Pass the richer feature matrix to all channels with common penalties Test whether more predictors help at all
3 Expanded linear PGARCH with channel-specific shrinkage Keep the richer feature matrix, but vary lambda_mu, lambda_phi, and lambda_g Test whether richer features help only when the channels are treated differently
4 Full XGBPGARCHModel preview Replace linear channel maps with boosted updates in \mu, \phi, and g Check whether the natural next step is a nonlinear channel update rather than another minor linear tweak

This roadmap separates three questions that are easy to blur together. The first is whether linear PGARCH benefits from broader information at all. The second is whether channel-specific allocation helps more than uniform expansion. The third is whether a boosted or nonlinear update becomes the right next branch once the linear expanded models stall.

4 Feature Universe and Taxonomy

The executable Part 6 snapshot contains a 54-column predictor matrix. It reuses the broader feature-engineering ideas developed earlier in the series, but the realized design in this notebook is the 54-column matrix produced by the Part 6 pipeline and local data availability.

The taxonomy below is organizational rather than architectural. In the current linear PGARCH implementation, every feature is passed to every channel. The taxonomy simply records which channel a feature class is most naturally associated with before shrinkage is applied.

Fast return and intraday measures are the natural candidates for g_t. Volatility-state variables such as HAR-style realized-volatility summaries, downside asymmetry, and volatility-of-volatility are the natural candidates for \phi_t. Slower state variables such as implied-volatility levels, cross-asset conditions, macro-release timing, and liquidity controls are most naturally associated with \mu_t.

That distinction is important for how the evidence should be read. The allocation table below is a prior about intended use, not proof that the fitted model enforces that allocation cleanly. One of the Part 6 findings is that shrinkage alone only partially succeeds at that task.

asym_df = pd.DataFrame(index=target_idx)

asym_df["neg_semi_rv_22"] = neg_rv

asym_df["downside_freq_22"] = neg_mask.rolling(22, min_periods=1).mean().shift(1)
asym_df = asym_df.fillna(0.0)
asym_cols = list(asym_df.columns)

rv_min_22 = rv_daily_vov.rolling(22, min_periods=5).min().shift(1)

def _assign_channel(feat_name):
    '''Assign a primary PGARCH channel based on the feature economic role.'''
    f = feat_name.lower()

    # g-channel: fast shock features
    g_features = {"overnight_return", "intraday_return", "overnight_sq", "intraday_sq",
                   "signed_rv_interaction"}
    if f in g_features:
        return "g (innovation)"
    if any(k in f for k in ["logret", "log_return"]):
        return "g (innovation)"

    # phi-channel: persistence features
    phi_features = {"rv_autocorr_5", "vov_22", "rv_range_22", "rv_ratio_5_22",
                    "neg_semi_rv_22", "pos_neg_rv_ratio", "downside_freq_22",
                    "rv_5", "rv_22", "rv_60", "rv_1"}
    if f in phi_features:
        return "phi (persistence)"

    # Everything else -> mu-channel
    return "mu (level)"


def _assign_group(feat_name):
    '''Assign a feature group label.'''
    f = feat_name.lower()
    if any(k in f for k in ["logret", "log_return"]):
        return "Return-based"
    if f.startswith("dow_"):
        return "Calendar (DOW)"
    if any(k in f for k in ["month_end", "quarter_end", "year_end"]):
        return "Calendar (Period-end)"
    if any(k in f for k in ["fomc", "opex", "imm"]):
        return "Calendar (Events)"
    if any(k in f for k in ["vix", "iv_rv", "vix3m"]):
        return "VIX / Implied Vol"
    if any(k in f for k in ["credit", "curve", "gold", "oil", "rate", "usd", "copper"]):
        return "Cross-Asset"
    if any(k in f for k in ["volume", "amihud"]):
        return "Volume / Liquidity"
    if any(k in f for k in ["gdp", "cpi", "nfp"]):
        return "Macro Release"
    if any(k in f for k in ["overnight", "intraday"]):
        return "Intraday Decomp"
    if any(k in f for k in ["rv_", "neg_semi", "downside", "signed_rv",
                             "vov_", "nfci", "stlfsi", "ted", "vvix"]):
        return "Volatility State"
    return "Other"

5 Evaluation Standard and Experimental Design

The dataset, target, and split are held fixed relative to Part 5. After the 200-row warm-up discard, the effective sample contains 3,800 training observations and 2,035 out-of-sample observations on SPY daily variance from 2000 through 2023. GARCH(1,1) remains the benchmark, and the Part 5 PGARCH-L baseline remains the active family reference.

The underlying model implementations live in the sibling volatility-forecast repository, principally volatility_forecast/model/pgarch_linear_model.py for the linear branch and volatility_forecast/model/xgb_pgarch_full_model.py for the nonlinear preview.

5.1 What Counts as Success

This series is not selecting models by in-sample fit. The decision rule remains the same as in Part 4 and Part 5: lower out-of-sample QLIKE than GARCH(1,1), with Diebold-Mariano tests on QLIKE loss used to judge whether a benchmark improvement is statistically credible. RMSE and in-sample QLIKE remain useful diagnostics, but they are secondary. A model that fits the training window better while losing on OS QLIKE or failing against the benchmark is not treated as a success.

7 Statistical Comparison

Point rankings are not enough on their own. The Diebold-Mariano table below checks whether the visible QLIKE differences are large enough to treat as more than noise.

In this notebook the DM evidence mostly reinforces, rather than overturns, the ranking table. The expanded all-feature models are not merely failing to post a benchmark-clearing p-value. On the most important comparisons, the loss differentials also point in the wrong direction.

Diebold-Mariano tests 
def _qlike_loss(y, yhat):
    y_a, h_a = np.asarray(y, float), np.maximum(np.asarray(yhat, float), 1e-12)
    return np.log(h_a) + y_a / h_a

def _sq_loss(y, yhat):
    return (np.asarray(y, float) - np.asarray(yhat, float)) ** 2

7.1 Reading the DM Tests

Three comparisons carry most of the interpretive weight.

First, Model C is worse than GARCH(1,1) on mean OS QLIKE, and its DM statistic against GARCH(1,1) is 1.5542 with p = 0.1203. That is not a benchmark-clearing result.

Second, Model C is worse than the Part 5 PGARCH-L baseline, and that comparison is stronger. The DM statistic against Model A is 1.9879 with p = 0.0470. The first structured all-feature specification is therefore not just inconclusive against the benchmark; it is materially worse than the simpler three-feature baseline under the Part 6 protocol.

Third, making the mu channel even looser is a negative result rather than an improvement. Model D is worse than Model C, with DM statistic 2.6157 and p = 0.0090. In this all-feature linear setup, extra freedom in the level channel amplifies overfitting rather than uncovering additional predictive signal.

The remaining rows are still useful. Uniform regularization does not improve on the Part 5 baseline, and relaxing the g penalty does not rescue the expanded design. The expanded linear branch therefore remains active as a modeling question, but not yet as a benchmark-clearing forecasting result.

def _extract_channel_coefs(model, feat_cols):
    '''Extract per-channel coefficients (on the standardised scale).'''
    theta = model.theta_
    block = len(theta) // 3
    # First element of each block is the intercept
    w_mu = theta[:block]
    w_phi = theta[block:2*block]
    w_g = theta[2*block:]
    # Skip intercept (index 0)
    return pd.DataFrame({
        "Feature": feat_cols,
        "Group": [_assign_group(f) for f in feat_cols],
        "Primary channel": [_assign_channel(f) for f in feat_cols],
        "w_mu": w_mu[1:],
        "w_phi": w_phi[1:],
        "w_g": w_g[1:],
        "|w_mu|": np.abs(w_mu[1:]),
        "|w_phi|": np.abs(w_phi[1:]),
        "|w_g|": np.abs(w_g[1:]),
    })

def _allocation_score(row):
    '''Fraction of total absolute weight in the primary channel.'''
    total = row["|w_mu|"] + row["|w_phi|"] + row["|w_g|"]
    if total < 1e-12:
        return np.nan
    ch_map = {"g (innovation)": "|w_g|", "phi (persistence)": "|w_phi|", "mu (level)": "|w_mu|"}
    primary_col = ch_map.get(row["Primary channel"], "|w_mu|")
    return row[primary_col] / total

coef_df["allocation_score"] = coef_df.apply(_allocation_score, axis=1)

11 What Part 6 Actually Learned

Part 6 leaves a cleaner result than the original draft suggested.

First, the Part 5 three-feature PGARCH-L baseline remains the strongest specification inside this notebook. It posts the best OS QLIKE among the evaluated fits.

Second, expanding the feature set inside linear PGARCH is not enough by itself. Models B through E all fit the training sample better than Model A, but each one loses ground out of sample. The Part 6 problem is therefore not lack of in-sample flexibility. It is controlling how richer information enters the recursion without paying too much variance.

Third, the structural story is still useful. The channel plots and coefficient tables indicate that richer predictors can move the level and persistence channels in economically interpretable ways, while the g_t channel becomes much less stable when its penalty is relaxed. That is not a forecasting win, but it is enough to keep channel-specific modeling alive as an active branch at this stage of the series.

Fourth, the nonlinear extension remains unresolved rather than rejected. The full boosted preview fails at initialization, which identifies a concrete technical obstacle and a natural next search step.

12 Limitations

The limitations are direct. Part 6 does not deliver an expanded-feature model that clears GARCH(1,1) on OS QLIKE. It also does not show that differential regularization alone can produce a clean channel allocation; the allocation-score table shows only partial steering, especially in the intended innovation channel. Finally, the nonlinear preview is incomplete because the initializer failed before a fair boosted comparison was available.

Those limitations are not incidental. They are the reason this post is an intermediate result rather than a final victory post.

13 Handoff to Part 7

Part 7 is therefore the broader search-and-stress-test phase, not a retroactive correction to Part 6. Part 6 leaves the series with a live but unresolved branch: richer PGARCH specifications remain plausible, but the all-feature linear versions overfit and the first full nonlinear preview is blocked by initialization.

The natural next search step is to keep the benchmark, target, and QLIKE-based decision rule fixed while broadening the specification search inside the PGARCH and XGBPGARCH line. That means testing whether the encouraging ideas from Part 6 survive alternative feature allocations, narrower screened subsets, better initialization discipline, and stronger robustness checks. In that sense Part 7 extends Part 6 rather than overturning it. The question shifts from whether richer structure can help in principle to which richer structure, if any, survives a wider and stricter search.
